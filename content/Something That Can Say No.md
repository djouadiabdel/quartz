---
title: Something That Can Say No
---

These eleven pieces are about a project rather than about a doctrine, and the subject is not Girard.

The subject is what it takes to build something that can tell you no.

René Girard is accused of having a theory that cannot lose. Hand a Girardian a text where the victim looks guilty and the mechanism explains it. Hand him one where the victim looks innocent and the mechanism explains that too. There is no text you could produce that would make him say *this one does not fit*. So I took the part of his theory that is enumerable, turned four of his sentences into word lists, and committed what those lists predicted to a public repository before writing the program that would score them.

Then I ran it, ten times, over three weeks. Fourteen predictions passed cleanly. Twenty-two failed. Seven falsifiers fired. Three studies were never run at all.

All of it is published, including the parts that make me look worse than saying nothing would have.

That is the whole reason these exist. A project of this shape that came back with twenty-two passes would tell you something got adjusted along the way, and you would be right to think so. The failures are not an embarrassment attached to the work. They are the only thing that makes the rest of it worth reading, and the most useful findings in the series are all failures of mine rather than results about Girard.

Nothing here argues that Christianity is true. The instrument compares vocabularies across texts, which is a question about texts.

## The wager, and the rule I had not read

- [[The Accusation and the Wager|The Accusation, and the Wager]], the objection stated at full strength, the one rule the whole project runs on, and the tally it has to live with.
- [[Girard in the Minimum]], the load-bearing quarter of the theory and nothing else, because only one part of it predicts anything you can count.
- **[[The Rule Girard Actually Wrote]]**, four pages into a chapter nobody in the project had opened, Girard states his own decision rule. It asks for less than we were testing, and the project's own numbers refute it in the other direction.

## Building the thing, and choosing what to point it at

- [[Turning Four Sentences Into Code]], why there is no stemming, the 960 hits I deleted from my own case, and the choice between adding four numbers up and taking the smallest.
- [[The Sample Is Part of the Claim]], a play scored with its sacrifice missing, a page that would have flipped a failed prediction and sits unused on disk, and a corpus that has four different sizes depending on which file you read.

## Where it broke, and what actually protects you

- **[[Four Broken Nulls One Mistake|Four Broken Nulls, One Mistake]]**, the only finding here that generalises past Girard entirely. Four rounds built a control test that held the wrong thing constant, and a three-row table would have caught every one.
- [[The Arithmetic All of It|The Arithmetic, All of It]], every statistic in the project, from a rate to a power calculation, with the picture always arriving before the formula.
- [[What a Timestamp Actually Proves]], the gaps between committing a prediction and committing its scorer are a median of six minutes, so the clock proves almost nothing. Something else does.

## What ten rounds produced

- **[[Ten Rounds and the Half That Never Carried Anything]]**, the half of the instrument that states Girard's actual claim has never carried a result on any measure in any round, and the reason is arithmetic anyone can follow.
- **[[A Check That Cannot Fail Prints Reassurance]]**, four checks built so that no possible result would make them complain. Three of them are mine.
- [[The Other Two Instruments]], the contradiction count, the resurrection control that is the best-designed thing in the repository, and what the whole thing cost.

## If you read four

The four in bold. They are where the actual findings are, and each one is checkable by somebody who does not trust me.

If you read one, read [[Four Broken Nulls One Mistake|Four Broken Nulls, One Mistake]]. It has nothing to do with Girard and you can use it on anybody's work, including your own.

## The sentence the whole thing turns on

> **A check that cannot fail prints reassurance.**

I built several without noticing. Not through carelessness, but through the ordinary process of writing a control, watching it pass, and moving on, which is what everybody does and is exactly the circumstance under which a broken control is invisible.

The only reason any of them were caught is a habit with no cleverness in it at all. Print the numbers next to the verdict, every time, so that a verdict which disagrees with its own evidence has somewhere to be noticed.
