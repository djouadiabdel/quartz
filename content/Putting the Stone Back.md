---
title: Putting the Stone Back
---

> *And when they had assembled with the elders and taken counsel, they gave a sufficient sum of money to the soldiers and said, "Tell people, 'His disciples came by night and stole him away while we were asleep.'"* (Matthew 28:12-13)

## The one thing he never does

I watched a debate this week between a Catholic named Trent and a very sharp young skeptic named Alex, on whether Jesus rose from the dead. Let me give Alex his due first, because he's honest in a way a lot of his side isn't. He keeps saying it out loud. "I won't say it didn't happen." "I'm not going to claim the body was stolen." "This is just a scenario that seems plausible to me." No theatrics. No sneering. A careful man being careful.

And that's exactly why I want to show you the trick underneath it, because it's a good trick, and you won't see it until somebody points.

Watch what Alex actually needs in order to win. He doesn't need to prove Jesus stayed dead. He never even tries. He only needs you to feel that the evidence fits the resurrection about as well as it fits some ordinary story. A tie. Because if it's a tie, a reasonable man doesn't move, and Alex gets to stand exactly where he was already standing, which is where he wanted to stand the whole time. He says it himself, in the clear: the data fits if it's true, "but it also fits with what would happen if it were not true." That's the entire game. Manufacture a tie at each point, and then call the tie a reason not to believe.

So here's the question to keep in your hand the whole way through, because it's the question he spends two hours avoiding. Not "can he poke a hole here." Of course he can poke a hole. Any smart man can poke a hole in anything that happened two thousand years ago. The question is: _what is his story?_ Not his objection. His story. Put it on the table next to mine, whole, and let's compare the two. He never does that. He pokes ten holes and never once sets down anything you could poke back. And there is a reason he won't, and by the end of this you're going to see that the reason is the oldest reason there is.

## Yesterday's camera

Start with the move he leans on hardest, the one about corroboration. He says, look, the reason we're all so sure Jesus even existed is that we have a pile of sources from outside the Bible, friends and enemies both, mentioning the man. But for the resurrection appearances? Nothing outside the Christian material. No neutral pen, no hostile pen, writing down that this man was seen alive again. And he says, if we just had one outside source, even a friendly one, that listed five hundred names and a place, he'd find that incredible.

It sounds fair. It's not. It's a camera from today held up to a world that had no camera.

Do this. Take any ordinary person from the year 30, a magistrate, a merchant, a teacher, anybody nobody was fighting over. Now go find me, from a neutral or hostile hand, a signed corroboration of one specific event in that person's life. You can't. Not for almost anyone who ever drew breath back then. The records simply do not exist, and the ones that do were written by people who cared. Or bring it closer, so it stings. Before there was an internet, before there were records that followed you around, go and prove that your great-great-grandmother existed and did the one thing your family says she did. What will you find? A few letters written by people who loved her. And nothing else. That is not a scandal. That is what the past is _made of_. And it is the exact evidential shape of the resurrection appearances: testimony written down by the people who were there and could not shut up about it.

Now here's the tell, and it's the thing that gives the whole game away. Alex imagines his perfect source, the five hundred names and the place. And in the same breath, before anyone even offers it to him, he's got it buried: well, whoever wrote it probably just read Paul. You see what happened? There is no piece of evidence you could hand him that survives contact with his hand. He has pre-refuted the thing he's asking for. And a standard that no possible evidence can ever satisfy is not a standard for weighing evidence. It is a wall built to keep a conclusion safe. Hold that thought, because we're going to come back to that wall and I'm going to tell you what it's really for.

## One cause, or a pile of escapes

Here's the move that should end the whole conversation, and almost nobody makes it, so make it.

Lay the facts out on the table, all at once, the ones even skeptical historians mostly grant. There's a tomb, and it's empty, or at least a body nobody can produce. There are people insisting they saw him, and not one or two, groups. There's a band of terrified men who ran and hid when he was arrested, and then weeks later are standing in the same city that just executed their leader, daring the authorities to do it to them too. There's an enemy, Paul, a man hunting these people down, who flips and spends the rest of his life getting beaten half to death for the very thing he was trying to stamp out. There's the leader's own brother, James, who thought he was a lunatic while he was alive, running the church after he's dead and getting stoned for it.

Now watch Alex handle them. The empty tomb? Maybe the body was moved, an emergency burial, then reburied. Or maybe stolen, there were grave robbers. The appearances? Visions, inner experiences, a flash of light. The martyrs? Well, we don't really know what any of them died for. Paul? A vision again. Notice what he's doing. He has a _different_ story for every single fact. A moved body over here. A hallucination over there. An unknown motive for the one, a light on the road for the other. Four, five, six little explanations, each one built to swallow one fact, none of them connected to any of the others.

That is not "the evidence fits if it's false." That is a man reaching into a different drawer for every problem. And every one of those little stories carries its own price. Grave robbers who happen to hit the one tomb everyone's watching. A mass hallucination that behaves like no hallucination on record, shared, group after group, and stubborn enough to march men to their deaths. A reburial that the reburiers, the very people who supposedly moved him, never once mention, not even to stop their friends from being killed over an empty hole they could have explained with one sentence.

Alex loves to talk about what makes a good explanation. So let's use his own rule. A good explanation is _one thing_ that accounts for _many things_. One cause, wide reach. That's what makes it good. And by that rule, the resurrection wins going away, because it is a single cause that predicts the whole list: empty tomb, appearances, nerve, the enemy convert, the brother, the movement that would not die. One cause. His account is not an explanation at all. It's a pile of unrelated escapes stacked in a heap and called a case. He is losing his own game and calling it a draw.

So the burden he keeps sliding out from under is this. It was never enough to say "here's another way that one fact could have happened." He owes us _one story_ that fits _all of them_ at once, and stands next to the resurrection, and beats it. He doesn't have one. He never puts one on the table. And now I'm going to tell you why he can't.

## The oldest trick in the world

Here we have to bring in a man named René Girard, because he saw something about human beings that unlocks this entire debate, and once you see it you can't unsee it.

Girard spent his life on one question: what is human culture built on? And his answer, worked out across book after book, was ugly and simple. It's built on a body. Some crisis breaks out, everyone against everyone, and the whole mob suddenly turns as one and falls on a single victim. They kill him. And here's the strange part, the killing works. The violence that was spread across everybody gets dumped onto one person, and when he's dead, the fever breaks. Peace comes. And the crowd, standing over the corpse, feeling that peace wash in, decides the dead man must have been the cause of it all. He was guilty. He was a monster, or a god, or both. And then they tell the story that way. The victim was dangerous, the crowd was right, order was restored.

That's what a myth is. It's the story of a lynching, told by the lynchers, arranged so the victim stays guilty and the crowd stays innocent. And the machine only runs on one fuel: concealment. It only works as long as nobody notices it was an innocent man and a guilty mob. The moment that gets out, the moment somebody says "wait, he did nothing, and we killed him for nothing," the spell breaks and the peace turns to ash. So the murder has to stay hidden. Girard took the name of his book straight from Scripture for this: the things hidden since the foundation of the world. What's been hidden? The body under the foundation. The founding murder every culture is standing on and has agreed not to see.

Now hold that up against the Gospels, because Girard's second thunderclap is this. The Gospels are made of the exact same material as the myths. A crisis. A mob. All of them against one. A killing that brings the city a grim kind of peace, Pilate and Herod made friends that day. Same lumber, every board of it. But the verdict is flipped. For the first time in the history of the world, the story of a lynching is told from the side of the _victim_, and the victim is innocent, and the crowd is wrong. "You killed the author of life." "Father, forgive them, they know not what they do." The Gospel takes the thing that had been hidden since the foundation of the world and drags it out into the daylight and names it. That's what Girard meant when he pointed at the cross and said this is where the machine gets exposed, this is Satan, the accuser, falling like lightning.

And the resurrection is the exposure finished. Think about what it does inside this frame. Every culture buries its victim, in the ground and under the lie. God does the opposite. He empties the grave and stands the innocent one back up, in public, and the very first sermon preached about it points at the crowd and says, _you_ did this, and _he_ was innocent, and God has overturned your verdict. The stone is rolled away. The hidden thing is unhidden. The victim will not stay buried and will not stay guilty.

Now. Look again at what the skeptic is doing, and you will never see this debate the same way.

He is not doing history. He is running the machine. Every single one of his moves is an attempt to get the victim back under the ground. The body was moved, put it back in a tomb. It was stolen, put it back in a tomb. They only saw a vision, then put the whole thing back inside a private head where it can't indict anybody. Each reconstruction is one more shovelful of dirt thrown back onto a man who won't stay covered. And you don't have to guess whether this is what's happening, because the very first response to the empty tomb, right there in the text, is exactly this move. Matthew tells us the guards were paid to say _his disciples came at night and stole the body._ The first thing the world did with an empty tomb was invent a story about a moved corpse. Two thousand years later Alex is saying the same sentence in a nicer accent. Emergency burial, reburial, grave robbers. It is Matthew 28 with footnotes. The crowd is still at the tomb, and the crowd still needs the body back.

And now, finally, we can answer his corroboration demand, the one about wanting an outside source, a neutral or a hostile one. Do you feel how strange that request is once you see the machine? He is asking the _crowd_ to corroborate the vindication of the man the crowd killed. He wants the persecutors to sign the confession. But Girard already told you: the record is always written by the mob, and the mob writes to protect the mob. There is no Theban city chronicle that says "we lynched an innocent Oedipus and we were wrong." There never is. The machine cannot say that sentence and keep running. So the reason there's no pagan document cheering the resurrection is not that it didn't happen. It's that the resurrection is precisely the one thing the machine exists to suppress. Asking for the crowd's corroboration is asking the machine to testify against itself.

And here's the knife. There _is_ one text that breaks ranks. One kind of document in all of human history that turns around and sides with the victim against the very crowd that produced it, that says the mob was wrong and the dead man was innocent and God raised him. It's the Gospel. That break is not a weakness in the evidence. That break is the fingerprint. It's the mark left behind when the machine, for the first time ever, failed to close. The absence Alex points at as a hole is exactly what you'd expect to find if it were true, and the one anomaly he waves away as "just apologetic material" is the single most unprecedented document the ancient world produced.

## Flesh, on pain of death

Let me take his sharpest small card, because it deserves a straight answer and it has one. Alex says: be careful about the martyrs. The persecution we can actually put our hands on is persecution for _visions_. Paul got a flash of light, not a body. Stephen died looking up at a vision in the sky. And for the men who might have touched something solid, like Peter, we don't independently know what they saw. So what did anyone really die for?

Two things.

First, Paul. Alex splits Paul's experience off as "only a vision," a category apart from the physical appearances. But that's Alex's split, not Paul's. Paul lists himself in the same breath as everyone else who saw the risen Lord, "last of all, he appeared also to me," and he asks, flat out, "have I not seen the Lord?" The witness himself refuses to file his experience in the drawer marked hallucination. You have to overrule Paul to make Paul say what Alex needs him to say. And that, if you've read the first half of this, should sound familiar. It's the priority of the conclusion over the text. Same move, different room.

Second, and this is the one to keep. Turn "it was only a vision" over and look at its underside, because spiritualizing the victim is just re-burial wearing a halo. Watch what "vision" actually does. It takes a real body, a real emptied grave, a real public overturning of the crowd's verdict, and it quietly relocates the whole thing inside someone's skull, where it can't accuse anyone and can't be checked by anyone. It's the gentlest way to put the stone back. Not "the body was stolen," just "it was all in their heads." Same result. The victim disappears from the world and reappears as a private feeling.

And the earliest voices after the apostles saw that move coming and threw their bodies in front of it. Take Ignatius. Bishop of Antioch, and tradition remembers him as a student of the apostles, a man who sat under John himself. And we have his letters, and we know exactly when he wrote them, because he wrote them on the road to Rome, in chains, being marched to the arena to be torn apart by animals. A man with days to live. And what does he spend that ink on? Not comfort. Not metaphor. He spends it hammering, over and over, that the risen Christ was _flesh_, because even then people were trying to make it a vision, and he calls that a heresy and dies rejecting it:

> For I know that after His resurrection also He was still possessed of flesh, and I believe that He is so now.

> Lay hold, handle Me, and see that I am not an incorporeal spirit.

> After His resurrection He ate and drank with them, as being possessed of flesh.

Read that and then read Alex's "it was only a vision," and feel the distance between them. A man walking to be eaten by lions does not spend his last words insisting on a body he could have spiritualized away to save himself the trouble. You spiritualize a metaphor. You die for a corpse that got up and ate breakfast. The very first thing the church did with the vision theory was refuse it, on the way to the grave, in the flesh.

## Putting the stone back

So step back and see the shape of the whole thing.

We built a piece a while ago on a simple principle: when a man's conclusion is fixed before he opens the book, and the words get "reinterpreted" every single time they cross him, the size of the reinterpreting is not a weakness in the text, it's a map. The bigger the wall, the more surely it marks the spot where the truth is trying to get through. We called it: the defense is the evidence.

This is that same principle, in a mirror. Alex's conclusion is fixed before he starts. Not the Qur'an this time, but naturalism, the flat rule that nothing like this can ever have happened. And watch the evidence get reinterpreted, every time, without exception, the instant it points at a risen man. Empty tomb, reinterpret it, the body was moved. Appearances, reinterpret them, visions. Martyrs, reinterpret them, we don't know what they died for. Enemy converts, an inner experience. Look at the height of that wall. Look at how much has to be explained away, how many separate little stories have to be kept standing at once. That wall is not covering a weakness in the resurrection. It is a map, and X marks the tomb. The defense is the evidence.

And under it all, Girard's old machine, still turning. Because this is what human beings do, it's what we have always done, from the foundation of the world. We find the innocent one, and we put him under the ground, and we tell the story so the crowd stays clean. The resurrection is the one time the body would not stay down. And ever since, the crowd has been standing at that open tomb doing the only thing it knows how to do, reaching for a story that puts the stone back. His disciples stole him. The gardener moved him. It was all in their heads. Emergency burial, grave robbers, a flash of light.

Two thousand years, and the stone keeps getting rolled away, and men keep showing up to roll it back. The next time you meet one of them, and he's careful, and he's sincere, and he says he just finds the mundane story a little more plausible, don't argue with the height of his wall. Look at it. And ask him the only question that matters.

What are you so determined to keep inside?
