---
title: The Night We Argued the Trinity
---


## How it started

It started as a vent, not a debate. A friend of mine, Salafi, had just burned an evening on apologetics videos and came out of it wrecked. Fourteen hours, he said, of some guy explaining how Jesus has every power God has, and then another guy calmly taking it apart with one line, that if you are in one place you cannot be everywhere, that if you are lower than someone at any moment then you are not God. One video says Jesus is God with a godly nature. The next says he is a sinless man who will stand beside God. And now, he told me, some writer wants me to believe A and not-A at once because that is how he decided to write it.

He was not being lazy. He had watched the whole thing, the contradiction he felt was real, and he was daring me to make it vanish with a clever word. There is nothing you could say, he wrote. No wording exists that makes that make sense.

So we went, most of a night. What follows is that argument, the objections that mattered and the answers that met them, in the order they fell. I am giving his case in its strongest form, because he deserves that and because the weak version teaches nothing. I should say at the top what this is not. It is not a knockout. It is a long retreat, his, from "this is logically impossible" to, in his own words at the end, "so we are just arguing nature versus a person," and the strange thing about that retreat is that most of the walls he backed into turned out to be built from his own bricks. But there were a few things near the end that did not retreat, and one under all of them I could not put down, and I have tried not to flinch from them, because they are the honest reason this stayed a conversation between friends and not a scalp.

---

## Act 1: The mirror in his own book

Before I defended anything, I handed him a mirror.

His own tradition holds that the Quran is the uncreated, eternal speech of God, and also a book of ink and paper you can print in a factory and burn in a fire. So I asked the question that shape forces on you. When your hand is on the mushaf, what are you touching. The uncreated Word of God. The created ink. Both.

This was not a trap for its own sake. It is the skeleton of the thing he was calling impossible. An eternal divine reality, the Word, the Logos, kalam Allah, arriving through a created vehicle, a body or a book, with a line drawn so that "eternal" and "created" do not collide on the same layer. The Ash'ari answer to the mushaf question is that you touch the created expression, not the eternal meaning. That is the same shape as the Christian saying Mary held the created human nature, not the divine nature that has no skin to touch.

He waved it off. You are treating the Quran like it is conscious, he said. Like it has a will. You are comparing a book to a person.

But the parallel was never about consciousness. It was about structure, eternal Word through created vehicle, split by level so it is not a formal contradiction. And here I want to be careful about what that mirror does and does not do, because it is easy to overclaim it. It dissolves the crude version of his objection, that this wording is A and not-A, self-cancelling on its face. It does not show that a person can be both God and a man who dies. That is a further and heavier thing. So what the mirror actually won was small and real. He could no longer say the mere structure is impossible, because he uses that structure every day for his own book. What survived, and what we spent the rest of the night on, was his better objection, the one he raised in the same breath. Yours is a person. He was right that this is the whole question. I just did not agree that raising it settled it in his favor.

---

## Act 2: The strawman, and the word "greater"

Then came the sentence he kept returning to for hours. Jesus is the Father himself, but also different. There is no wording, he said, that makes that anything but A and not-A.

Here naming things is mercy, not pedantry, because the thing he was swinging at is a heresy Christians threw out seventeen centuries ago. The claim that Jesus is the Father, two masks on one person, has a name. Modalism. Sabellianism, after Sabellius, who was excommunicated for it. Patripassianism, the idea that the Father is the one who bled on the cross. The Church did not tolerate it. So a good part of his fury was aimed at a doctrine no Christian holds.

The real claim splits two words he had fused. One "what," one single divine being. Three "whos," Father, Son, Spirit. Jesus is not the Father. He is a distinct person who is the one God.

And there is a strand in the Hebrew scripture, the one his own Quran tells him to honor, that at least runs with this rather than against it. The strongest case is the Angel of Yahweh, a figure who is called Yahweh, speaks as "I AM," accepts worship, and is still spoken of as sent by Yahweh, both identified with God and distinguished from him in the same passages. Genesis 19:24, where in the Hebrew Yahweh rains fire from Yahweh, belongs in the same family. Genesis 1:26, "let us make mankind in our image," is the weakest of the three, because it has stock readings a Jewish or Muslim reader will offer at once, a plural of majesty or God addressing his heavenly court, and I will not pretend those are stupid. So I do not say these verses settle anything. I say the later doctrine claims to be the fulfillment of a pattern that is already there, and the Angel is the piece that is hard to file away.

He reached, correctly, for the other cliff. So, three gods. Which is why the discipline never changes. One what, three whos. Not three god-natures in a row. One numerically single divine being.

Then his best early punch, and it is real. If one is stronger or higher than the other at any moment, even for a second, the second one is not God.

A fair word before I lean on John, because his tradition does not simply hand me this Gospel. A Salafi can say, and usually does, that these are not the words of Jesus but a later Christian hand, tahrif. He did not say it that night. He granted me the text and fought me on what it means rather than whether it is genuine, and I took the courtesy and built on it. So everything I draw from John from here stands inside that grant, not outside it, and I come back at the end to what the grant costs.

Scripture does not make this easy. Jesus says "the Father is greater than I" (John 14:28, NIV). He prays. He submits. He says not even the Son knows the hour (Mark 13:32, NIV).

It turns on the word greater. The Greek is meizon, greater in rank or position, not in nature. A general outranks a private who is exactly as human as he is. And the same author who records "the Father is greater" also records "I and the Father are one" (John 10:30, NIV) and "anyone who has seen me has seen the Father" (John 14:9, NIV). He is holding two axes at once, equal in nature, ordered in role. Philippians 2:6-7 (NIV) names the mechanism. Being in very nature God, he made himself nothing, taking the very nature of a servant. The load-bearing word there, rendered "very nature" by the NIV, is morphe, form, and the argument does not ride on the gloss. The verse sets the form of God against the form of a servant in the same breath, and the servitude is plainly something he takes on rather than something he always was. That survives whichever translation you hold.

And the line spoken in the same breath as the lowliness, "glorify me in your presence with the glory I had with you before the world began" (John 17:5, NIV). A prophet does not have glory with God before the universe exists. So either he is speaking falsely, and Islam has lost a true prophet, or he is the pre-existent Son coming home. He felt the pinch of that one.

---

## Act 3: The Word that pleads

I mentioned that the Quran itself intercedes. There is a hadith where the Quran comes on the Day of Resurrection and pleads, yashfa'u, for the one who recited it. So I asked how the Word of God pleads with God.

He had a sharp answer ready. A deed can intercede too. On that Day your own hands and skin will speak, and that does not make your hand a mind. God can give speech to anything. It says nothing about what the thing is.

He was switching two categories, and the honest move is to separate them slowly. The hand that speaks is shahada, testimony, forced and neutral, reporting a fact against you. A witness box. What the Quran does is shafa'a, intercession, and that is a different act. An intercessor does not report facts. He takes your side and asks the Judge for your acquittal. That is a defense lawyer, not evidence.

And the source cuts his way less than he thinks. In the hadith the Quran does not sit there being tallied. It turns to God, says "O Lord," speaks in the first person, builds a case, and asks for a verdict. That is a party standing before God, calling itself "I," pleading for a man. Which leaves him a fork. Either the Word of God really stands before God and pleads, which is a distinction inside the one God, or the whole scene is metaphor, in which case he is reading his own scripture as figure the moment it presses on him. I do not think either door is comfortable for his position. The mirror on my side is plain enough. Christ "always lives to intercede" for us (Hebrews 7:25, NIV), and "we have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ" (1 John 2:1, NIV).

He answered that God can animate anything on that Day without it reflecting the thing's nature, or else everything is conscious. I granted that at once, because it is true. And then two things had quietly happened to his own position. He had dropped his consciousness objection from Act 1, which means he can no longer bar Jesus from the divine nature just for having a mind and a will. And he had described the two-natures doctrine in his own words, that what a thing is made to do has nothing to do with its original state. Original state, the nature. What it does in a given mode, the role. He grants that split for the Quran, for hands, for objects, for every created thing. The question I was left with, and left him with, was why it is forbidden for exactly one person.

---

## Act 4: The Salafi ground, and his own attributes

He restated the dilemma. Either Jesus is the Father, one single God, or he differs in any way at all, and then they are two gods.

I refused both horns, because the middle is not a wall. Not a different entity. Not "a" god, one item on a shelf. A different person, in the one being of the one God. His menu was rigged, modalism on the left, tritheism on the right, and the door between them treated as if it were not there.

Then the move that ends this round, using his own materials. Salafi creed affirms that God has real, distinct, eternal attributes, His Knowledge, His Power, His Life, His Speech, "not Him and not other than Him." His Knowledge is not His Power. They are genuinely distinct, all eternal, all uncreated, all fully divine, and that is not a pantheon. It is one God. So he already holds distinction inside the one God that is not several gods. He calls it tawhid.

But the parallel proves less than it looks, and I leaned on it too hard at first. It proves the structure. It shows that "really distinct, and still one God" is a shape he already accepts, so distinction-in-God is not by itself polytheism, and the counting objection is dead. It does not prove that the distinct thing can be a person. An attribute is not a someone. God's knowledge does not get sent, does not become flesh, does not pray, does not receive worship. So the parallel clears the ground and then stops at the edge of the real question. The Son is claimed to be more than an attribute, a "who," and everything that makes my friend uneasy lives in that "more." I can say, correctly, that his own theology proves distinction and unity can live together. I cannot pretend that settles whether one of those distinctions is a man who dies. It does not, and I told him so.

On the same ground I owe an honest word about his scripture, because the verse that names Jesus the Word cuts both ways and I will not quote only the half I like. Q 4:171 does call Jesus kalimatullah, the Word of God, and ruh minhu, a Spirit from Him. It also, in the same verse, says do not say "Three," and says God is one, exalted above having a son. I am not going to hide the second half behind the first. My reading, and I grant it is a reading and not the plain sense, is that the "Three" it forbids, taken with Q 5:116, is Father, Mary, and Jesus, a family of gods I reject as fully as he does, and that the "son" it denies is the physical walad, a child by a consort, which I also deny. He can fairly answer that the verse names no third party and means to forbid any three, mine included, and I do not get to wave that away. What is left standing, on any reading, is that his own book reaches for its highest vocabulary, Word and Spirit, and lays it on Jesus and on no other prophet. That is not a proof. It is a datum his tradition has had to work hard to hold.

And there is one internal pressure I did lean on, fairly I think, because it is his own school's hero and not mine. To deny a real feature of God because reason cannot picture it has a name in his tradition, ta'til, and his creed exists to condemn it. Ahmad ibn Hanbal was jailed and flogged in the Mihna for defending the real, uncreated Speech of God against exactly that reasoning. And Ibn Taymiyya, his own Shaykh al-Islam, did not dismiss the Christian attributes argument. He wrote volumes against it, and conceded before he disputed that God truly has a Word and a Life. His own method affirms that God has a Hand, rose over the Throne, descends each night, all bila kayf, held without asking how. I only asked him to notice that he had denied the Trinity for the one reason his own method forbids, that his intellect stalled.

---

## Act 5: The logician's trap, Leibniz, and one will

This is where the sharper polemic lives, the version with a little philosophy in it, and he found his way there.

Start with a fair question. When you say "Jesus is God," is that identity or predication. Identity would make Jesus and God interchangeable in every sentence. Predication means Jesus has the divine nature. I said predication, because "Jesus is God" and "the Father is God" cannot both be identities without collapsing the Son into the Father.

Then the trap. If it is predication, he said, the Father is one center of consciousness and the Son is another, so two gods. And if you say they are one, you have broken Leibniz's law, that truly identical things share every property, since the Father is unbegotten and the Son is not. To hold the Trinity you have to throw out a pillar of logic.

Much of that is bluff, and it is worth being exact about why. Leibniz's law governs absolute identity, and I do not claim the Father is absolutely identical to the Son. I claim the opposite. They differ, really, by relation. So the law is not broken, it is simply not what I asserted. What is one is the essence. What is three is the persons. The one thing each person is strictly identical to is the single divine essence, which each wholly is, and there the law holds without a scratch, because there is only one essence to be identical to.

I have to be careful here, because this is where the essay could quietly contradict itself, and a good reader would catch it. To answer the two-consciousness worry I say there is one divine mind and one divine will, belonging to the nature, shared by the three persons. But in Act 2 I leaned on a Son who prays, submits, and does not know the hour, which sounds like a second will. A careful Muslim puts those side by side and asks which is it. The answer is the old one the councils reached, and I should say it plainly rather than let the seam show. There are two wills in the incarnate Christ, a divine will and a human will, because in the incarnation the Son takes on a complete human nature, and a real human nature has a real human will. The one will that answers the two-gods worry is the single divine will the three persons share. Gethsemane, "not my will but yours," is the human will of the incarnate Son bowing to that divine will. So the "three" is never three divine wills. The prayer and the submission and the ignorance of the hour belong to the human nature he assumed. Without that distinction my two answers fight each other. With it they do not.

As for the ship analogy people reach for here, it earns one thing and no more. The Titanic that sailed and the wreck on the seabed are not identical in every property, one is whole and dry and one is broken and drowned, yet everyone calls them the same ship. That is worth exactly one thing and no more. It shows that Leibniz's strict identity is too strong even for a boat, that our ordinary word "same" already survives real difference, so demanding absolute identity everywhere is not the neutral logic he pretends it is. It does not model the Trinity. A ship changing over time is one thing across a span of time. Three persons in one being is not that, and I will not smuggle a picture of change-over-time in as if it proved three-at-once. It clears his overreach. It does not build the doctrine.

---

## Act 6: What being even means

He stopped and asked the honest question under all of it. What is the actual difference between one being and one person. Say it plainly.

Being is the state of existing. A chair has being, it exists. A rock has being. A bottle on the table has being. Everything that is, has being. But not everything that has being is a person. If I start talking to my bottle, scolding it, someone should come take me somewhere quiet, because the bottle exists and yet no one is home. Being answers the question what. Person answers the question who, the one who has a mind, who wills and loves and is aware that he exists and that you do too. A chair is a what with no who. You are a what with exactly one who.

And there is the whole trip-wire. For us, what and who come one to one, always. You have never met a being that was more than one person, so your mind signs a law it was never handed, that one being must mean one person, everywhere, for anything. Then you carry that law, built entirely out of being a creature, and press it onto God. That is the error. Not stupidity, just a creature reaching for the only ruler it owns and trying to measure the uncreated with it.

Scripture names the move and forbids it. "To whom will you compare me. Or who is my equal," says the Holy One (Isaiah 40:25, NIV). "Who in the skies above can compare with the LORD" (Psalm 89:6, NIV). No one, nothing in the created order is built the way God is built. So "you are one being and one person, therefore God must be" quietly puts God on the one shelf he said he is not on. The Shema, "the LORD our God, the LORD is one" (Deuteronomy 6:4, NIV), says there is one God, which I confess with him. I will not hang weight on the old apologetic claim that the word for one there secretly means composite unity. It does not carry that load, the word is just "one," and a careful reader knows it. The Shema says one God. It does not, by itself, decide whether that one God is single-personal or not. That is the very thing we were arguing.

So the plain answer to his plain question. Different in person, one in being. One "what," more than one "who." And when he pushed, then what makes Jesus not the Father, the smallest and hardest thing in the doctrine. Jesus comes from the Father. That is the whole difference. Not a separate mind, not a separate will, not a separate anything he owns. A direction. A source and what flows from it can never be the same one, because nothing is its own source. Not a possession. A relationship, and a relationship needs two ends.

---

## Act 7: Generated, caused, dependent

Now the hardest attack, the one the better polemicists save to finish with, and he found it through al-Ikhlas.

You say the Son is eternally generated, begotten from the Father. Generated means caused. Caused means dependent, contingent, a being that leans on another to exist. And al-Ikhlas says God is as-Samad, the Self-Sufficient, who depends on none, and that he does not beget nor is begotten. So a generated, dependent Son is a creature with a nicer name. And if you say he is caused and uncaused at once, you have not solved it, you have walked out of logic and broken non-contradiction. A necessary being cannot be dependent in any respect, not one percent.

It is a good argument, and it deserves the real answer.

The word caused is doing two jobs, so pull them apart. One, caused in your being, sourced in some other being outside yourself, made to exist by what is not you. That is what makes a creature a creature. Two, receiving the one divine essence from the Father within the one God, where the Father is not another being, not something outside, but the same single God. The first is dependency. The second is a relation of origin inside one being. The Son is not sourced in another essence. Nothing external holds him up. He needs no world, no ground outside God, because there is no outside God for him to lean on. As-Samad describes the essence, and the Son wholly is that essence, so he is exactly as independent of everything created as the Father.

The contradiction charge only works if one word keeps one meaning in both slots, and it does not. A contradiction is A and not-A in the same respect. The Son is caused hypostatically, as a relation to the Father, the way a mind's word is from the mind. He is uncaused essentially, because his being is not derived from any other being. Different respects, no contradiction. Flatten the two senses into one word and of course it looks broken. That is an equivocation to refuse, not a law of logic to fear.

And al-Ikhlas never reached this. Lam yalid wa lam yulad forbids physical begetting, a god who takes a consort and produces offspring, a dependent son born in time, the thing the pagan Arabs said when they called the angels God's daughters. Christians deny that as hard as any Muslim. Eternal generation has no consort, no division, no beginning, no second being. As-Samad is not threatened by a God who has his Word and his Spirit eternally within himself. It is threatened by a solitary point that would need a creation to have anything to know or love. So when he thundered that a necessary being cannot depend on anything in any respect, I agreed with him and took the weapon. The Son depends on nothing outside God. The only "from" in him runs to the Father, and the Father is God.

---

## Act 8: One holder, and the finish line

Late and tired, he found the strongest classical argument for God's uniqueness and sharpened it clean. The divine nature cannot be shared, because God is complete, and two different things cannot both be complete. Finer still, any difference means one is more and one is less, even by an atom, so one is God and one is not.

The buried premise is that every difference is a difference of amount. It is not. Complete means having every perfection, all power, all knowledge, all being, missing no good thing. It does not mean having every property, including every relation. The Son has every perfection and lacks none. The one thing he does not have is "being the source," and that is a relation, not a perfection. Then the reductio, run both ways. The Son lacks "being the source." But the Father lacks "being the Son." Each lacks the other's relation. So by his own rule the Father is not God either. The rule leaves zero gods standing, not one, which means it is not a discovery of the one true God, it is a broken rule. There are not two completenesses fighting for a throne. There is one, and both persons wholly are it, tied on every perfection, differing only in which way the "from" runs.

Which became his final thesis. The nature of God accepts only one holder. Or else, he asked, what separates you from polytheism.

The trap is in the word holder, because it forks between "being" and "person." One being, one entity, I grant, that is tawhid, and I never said otherwise. One person is exactly the thing he has to prove, and calling it the definition assumes the conclusion. Every proof he had reached "one being" and stopped there. The classic Islamic proof, tamanu' from Q 21:22, that two gods with two wills would deadlock and the cosmos would fall apart, needs two wills, and there is one divine will, shared. It annihilates two separate gods, which I also reject. It does not touch one God in three persons.

And then, worn down, he said it himself. So we are still just arguing nature versus a person. Yes. But I want to be honest about what arriving there is and is not, because it is tempting to call it a win and it is not one. Reaching the real question is not answering it. Everything I did that night showed that his objection is not a counting problem and not a bare contradiction. It did not show why the distinct "who" should be a person at all rather than merely an attribute, and it did not show that a person of God can be a man who is worshipped and dies. Those are the mountain. I had cleared the foothills and pointed at the mountain, and there is a difference.

---

## The shape of it

I do not want to lay his night out as a scoreboard, because it was not a rout, whatever the shape suggests. Most of my answers were the best available rather than the last word, and several are contested by people who have worked at this longer than either of us. But the shape is worth naming once. He began at "this is logically impossible" and ended at "so we are just arguing nature versus a person," and nearly every step between was the same motion. Any distinction must be two gods, then any difference must make one lesser, then the divine nature can hold only one holder, and each claim fell back when it turned out to rest on a rule he does not actually keep, because his own doctrine of the attributes already breaks it. What that leaves is narrower and stranger than where we started. For most of a night he had been defending monotheism against a polytheist who was not in the room. We are both monotheists. The shirk-by-counting charge dies the moment we both say one God. What we actually disagree about turned out to be smaller and sharper than either of us thought when we started shouting.

---

## The one that did not go down

But some things did not retreat, and the honest version of this night has to end on them, not on the tally.

The first he put better than I can. Fine, he said, grant your one being and your three persons, grant all of it. You still bow to a man. You worship someone who ate, who wept, who bled, who died. In my religion the gravest sin there is directing worship to other than God, shirk in ibada, and no diagram about persons and essences touches that. You kneel to a crucified man and call him Lord.

On my own premises I have an answer, and it is real. Worship of the Son is worship of the one God, because the Son is not other than the one God, so it does not land on a creature. But notice what that answer is. It is only good if the Son is in fact the one God. On his premise, that Jesus is a man, kneeling to him is exactly the blasphemy he says it is. So the objection does not add a new argument. It restates the whole disagreement in its most naked and most serious form, and it points, once more, at the person question I had cleared the ground around but not conquered.

The second is older and harder still. If the Son is God, who held the cosmos together during the three days the body lay in the tomb. How does the deathless die. I can give the technical answer. The divine nature does not die, the person dies in the human nature he took on, the incarnation is an addition and not a subtraction, so in him all things held together the whole time (Colossians 1:17, NIV). That satisfies the logician in me. It does not dissolve the scandal, and I have stopped pretending it should. Paul did not call the cross a difficulty to be cleared up. He called it a stumbling block and foolishness (1 Corinthians 1:23, NIV), and then he preached it anyway.

The third is quieter than the other two, and I set it aside all night only because he let me, and I have to name it or I am cheating the ledger. Every line of John I leaned on, in Act two and after, he could have refused outright as not the words of Jesus but a later hand, tahrif. He granted me the text. Hand it back to him for a moment and the pre-existence saying in John 17:5, which I turned into a clean two-door fork, stops being clean, because there is a third door, that Jesus never said it. I cannot shut that door in one night or one essay, and I will not pretend the night shut it. It is the floor under the floor we were standing on, and we both knew it was there, and we both agreed, for a while, to stand on the boards above it.

So here is where the arguing stopped and something else started. I had shown, I think, that the Trinity is not a contradiction and not a counting error, that most of the walls my friend hit were his own, and that what is left standing is a short stack of honest things, and under all of them one question, whether this particular man is the one God. Nothing in the ontology answers that question. It only clears away the reasons for refusing to ask it. Whether you kneel is decided by who you think he is, and that is not a proof you can force on anyone, including yourself.

I have seen the other way this goes, the stage version, a big man in a chair calling the other one unqualified, mocking his teeth, talking over every sentence, declaring victory into a cheering room while the crowd goes home fed on a humiliation instead of an answer. That is not what this was, and I do not think it is what wins anything worth keeping. This was two friends, one of them tired and honest and swinging as hard as he could, the other just refusing to let a real question be shouted down. He moved his ground all night as he found out the walls were his, which is not weakness, it is the rarest courage in an argument. And at the end I was not standing over him. I was standing next to him at the foot of the one thing I cannot argue anyone across, telling him the truth, that I did not solve the scandal, I knelt before it, and that I could show him everything except the last step, which no one can take for another.

## Sources and further reading

The move in Act 4, where a doctrine gets protected by bending the text that threatens it, is the same move I take apart at length in [[The Defense Is the Evidence]]. The patristic spine, Gregory of Nazianzus, Theological Orations 29 and 30, for "greater as cause, equal in nature." Gregory of Nyssa, On Not Three Gods, for distinct persons who are one God. Augustine, On the Trinity, Books 5 to 7, for persons as relations. Athanasius against the Arians for a generation that is not a creation. The Third Council of Constantinople, 681, for the two wills of Christ. And the creed itself, from Nicaea, true God from true God, Light from Light, begotten not made.
