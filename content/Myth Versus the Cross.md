title: Myth Versus the Cross

*Case File 03. The verdict.*

Strip two accounts down to their frame and only one line ever changes. This file is an exercise in stripping.

## The same parts

Take a myth, any myth with a killing in it, and take the execution outside Jerusalem, and reduce both to the components a structural engineer would care about. Not the theology, not the poetry, not the names. The load-bearing parts.

A crisis that has the community frightened.

A crowd that becomes one thing, which is the part people underestimate. A crowd in this state is not a collection of individuals who happen to agree. It is a single body with a single object, and the unanimity is the point of it. This is why these accounts so often stress that everyone was involved.

A victim, selectable by some mark.

A killing, and the killing is public. Not a murder in the dark. A rite, with an audience.

And afterward, a peace. The community is calmer than it was. This is the detail that makes the whole thing durable, because it is the reward, and it is real. Something did resolve.

Both accounts have every one of those parts. Not similar parts. The same parts, in the same order, doing the same structural work. You could build either one from the same kit.

## The line that changes

So write out the verdict each account returns. Both are short.

A myth says: victim guilty, crowd right.

That is the finding of every myth there is. It is stated in a hundred registers, sometimes with pity, sometimes with horror, sometimes with genuine tenderness toward the one who died. But the finding does not vary. The dead man brought the crisis. Removing him ended it. The community, whatever its excesses in the moment, was correct about the essential thing.

This one says: victim innocent, crowd wrong.

Same event, same structure, opposite verdict.

## Why it is nine words

I want to be exact about how narrow this is, because the narrowness is the whole finding.

The difference is not that one account is more humane. Myths are frequently humane about their victims. It is not that one has a god in it and one does not. Both do. It is not that one is older or better attested or better written.

The two accounts differ in one place: who is guilty. Everything else is held constant. Nine words carry the entire distance between them, and the nine words are a verdict, not a description.

## What that verdict costs

Notice what the second finding does to the story it belongs to.

A myth that returns the first verdict has a hero, and the hero is the community. The account is told by the crowd, about the crowd, and it clears the crowd. That is a functional document. It lets people live with themselves and it lets the peace hold.

Reverse the verdict and the crowd is no longer the hero of its own story. The people telling the account are now the ones who got it wrong, and they are saying so, in the document they organized their common life around. They have written down that their unanimity, the very thing that produced the peace, was a mistake, and that the peace was bought by killing someone who had done nothing.

That is not a small edit. It removes the reason the account was worth keeping.

## The question

A culture writing about a killing it committed has one obvious side to take, and it is not the side of the corpse.

Ask what kind of event forces a culture to change sides. To stop identifying with the crowd it belongs to and start identifying with the one it killed.

Every myth sides with the mob. One does not. Why?
