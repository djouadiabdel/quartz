---
title: The Frame and the Rival
---

The Bible handles polygamy in a manner that is never argumentative in a direct sense. Rather, it just tells stories about it.

It never forbids it, never recommends it at all, and through those stories you can see the general picture. You can understand human desire working in real time, through the lens of human experience.

The fight over polygamy was always a grammar fight. Is *heis* a cardinal or an ordinal? Does *gamous* being plural mean Christ has a harem? Does *idios* imply ownership? You can spend two hours there and win every skirmish and still miss the war, because the case for monogamy was never really a case about Greek. It is a case about how desire works, and about what a frame is for.

Two men can help us see it better than a hundred proof texts, René Girard and G.K. Chesterton.

## The household and the marriage of flesh as a rivalry

Girard's one great idea is that we do not desire things directly. We desire them because someone else desires them. Desire is not a straight line from me to the object. It is a triangle of me, the object, and a model who shows me the object is worth wanting. And the trouble with the triangle is that the model, the one who taught me to want, inevitably becomes my rival for the very thing he taught me to want, as we compete for the same resources.

This takes us to the tenth commandment, telling us never to covet (to desire) the belongings of another, and to Leviticus, telling us to love our neighbours as ourselves. Not more and not less, but as ourselves, because the Author understands desire.

Now look at what polygamy actually builds. It takes this triangle and pours the foundations of a house on top of it.

Sarah and Hagar. Leah and Rachel. Hannah and Peninnah. The Bible does not have to editorialize, because the structure editorializes itself. Two women, one man, one supply of love and status and inheritance that cannot be divided without being diminished. The wives become what Girard calls doubles, mirror images locked onto the same scarce object, each defined by her rivalry with the other.

Leah's whole identity curdles into the competition. The harshest verse in the Torah is the one where Leah buys a night with her own husband from Rachel, paying in mandrakes her son found in a field. That is not a marriage. That is an auction, and the lot on sale is a man's attention.

"The husband becomes a central scarcity."

Yes. He becomes the scarce object at the centre of converging desire. The wives orbit him as rivals, and the sons orbit the wives as heirs, and the whole system runs on a fuel that is never in short supply.

Envy.

And envy, in Girard, always ends the same way. It needs a victim. It finds one.

## Joseph in the pit

Jacob loved Rachel and merely had Leah. He loved Rachel's son and merely tolerated the rest. So when Esau's army looms, he lines his household up as a human shield, the unloved wives and their children in front, Rachel and Joseph in the safe rear. The favoritism is not a personal failing that happens to occur inside a polygamous house. It is the output of a polygamous house. Where love is divided unequally among rivals, someone is always at the front of the line.

Joseph is the reason his brothers can be read as a Girardian crowd.

Eleven doubles, unanimous in their hatred, throwing the favoured one into a pit and selling him into the dark. He is the scapegoat, expelled so the group can hold together, and the entire remaining book exists to vindicate him, to declare the cast-out one innocent and, in fact, the saviour.

Scripture does not moralize about polygamy from the outside. It talks about it from the inside. It does not act like a silent watcher, or a ruler sitting on his mighty throne casting judgement on the wicked, because it is doing something more radical. From the inside, it is siding with the victim the system produces.

It is showing us that the more people desire the same thing, the more they are pitted into rivalry, which leads to the community looking for a solution, someone to expel, because it is always about violence. The violent self will always pick violence to solve its problem, and in Joseph's story they picked the pit.

It does not lecture Hagar's master. It goes with Hagar into the wilderness, and it names her son Ishmael, "God hears," precisely because everyone else had stopped listening, but God had not, and he never stops listening to us. The text does not wag a finger at the mechanism. As the Author, God knows he ought to do something rather different from what a mere mortal would do in his text. He exposes the mechanism by refusing to look away from who it crushes.

So when someone says, "Well, God never called it sin," they have read the silence backwards. The silence is not permission. The silence is never an argument for permission. The silence is a camera staying and staring at the pain of a broken family and a broken household.

The most beautiful part of the picture is the frame, and that was Girard's idea too. The stories produce the scene, and the scene is the warning for those who see and hear.

## The frame and the fence

Now bring in Chesterton, because Girard tells us what polygamy breaks and Chesterton tells us what monogamy is, and it is the opposite of what the polygamist thinks it is.

The polygamist argues from abundance. Imagine a thousand wives. Imagine the fullness, the plenty, the sheer generous scale of it. More love, more life, more. It sounds like the larger, braver thing, and monogamy sounds like a small man's ration, a mere small thing a man gets when he is a nobody.

But a painting is not enlarged by tearing off its frame. It is destroyed, and the paint runs off into the wall. The man who wants "more" by taking a second wife is the man who thinks he can improve a poem by adding more words and adjectives. He gets more words and no poem.

This is Chesterton's paradox turned on the harem fantasy. The wild, adventurous, romantic thing is not variety. Variety is the jaded thing, the bored appetite that mistakes accumulation for abundance.

The genuinely daring act, the "rash vow," as he called it, is to stake everything on one face, to say this one, and no reserve, and no exit. Fidelity is not the safe choice. It is the duel to the death that no man of honour should decline. The frame is not smaller than the wilderness. The frame is where the whole world gets in.

Just as Christ was one with the Church and suffered for her, he showed us love, and what love is meant to be.

And there is Chesterton's fence, the parable made for this exact argument. A man finds a fence across a field and says, "I see no use in this, let us clear it away." Chesterton's reply, go away and find out why it was put there, and come back when you can tell me.

Monogamy is that fence, guarding us from something we would otherwise meet on a daily basis, something that would make life horrendous for all of us. The polygamist stands before it declaring it a mere cultural relic, a rabbinic accretion, a translator's conspiracy. And the reason it was built is precisely what Girard exhumed. The fence keeps the rivalry-engine outside the house. Pull it down and you do not get more garden. You get the doubles, the auction, the pit.

## Why it becomes a movement

One last thing the two lenses explain together, which the moralizing lens cannot. Why is this a movement, a thing people catch, argue into, evangelize for online?

Because the desire was never really for the women. If it were, it would be quiet and particular.

A movement runs on mimetic contagion. People want it because the men they admire in the movement want it. The model's desire is the actual object. That is why it comes bundled with grammar-charts and Dead Sea Scroll citations and a whole apologetic apparatus. The elaborate scaffolding is the tell.

Nobody builds a cathedral of footnotes around a desire that is simply, restfully theirs. They build it around a desire they borrowed, and have to keep justifying, because it belongs to someone else and points at nothing.

Girard had a word for the thing that fascinates you, that you cannot stop circling, that you mistake for freedom while it slowly enslaves you.

Skandalon.

The stumbling-block. That is the shape of the whole enterprise. It presents itself as liberation and functions as an obsession.

## Two become one

Set the triangle aside, and let us talk about marriage.

The two shall become one flesh. Not three, not five, not a thousand. A closed dyad, with no rival vertex, no scarce centre, no front of the line.

Two people pouring into the same glass, together putting their souls in it for God. Not a transaction, not a push toward an auction, and not something that creates rivalry when it is done as it was meant to be.

It is the only human arrangement that structurally cannot manufacture a scapegoat, because there is no third party for the rivalry to converge on and no surplus of doubles to expel. Monogamy is not the rule that spoils the party. It is the one frame in which love stops needing a victim.

And here is where bringing a child forth can create that very rivalry.

The child is never the cause of the rivalry. The child is the point where existing desire crystallizes into something inheritable. In a house ordered rightly, the child is received as gift. In a house built on rivalry, the child is born as a stake, a piece already on the board. Polygamy is the structure that turns every gift into a stake.

But that is a reckoning of its own. [[The Detonator]]
