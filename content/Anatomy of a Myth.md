title: Anatomy of a Myth

These five pieces are a different kind of writing than the rest of this site, and the difference is deliberate.

Everywhere else here I argue. I say what I think the cross is and why, I name Girard, I quote Paul, I tell you where I land. These do none of that. Each one takes a question you can ask about any ancient text, works it out on neutral ground where nothing is at stake, and then runs the same test on one more case. Then it stops. It does not tell you what the result means. It ends on a question and leaves you holding it.

I did it this way because the ordinary apologetic move gives the game away in the first sentence. The moment a reader knows the verdict you want, everything after it reads as advocacy, and the strongest thing in this material is not an argument at all. It is a pattern anyone can check. A pattern is worth more when the reader finds it than when I hand it over.

So none of these five will ever say the word Christian, and none of them will tell you the cross is different. Four of them will show you a test. One of them will show you what happens when you run it.

## The case files

- [[Detecting a Doctored Record]], how you spot a story that has been rewritten, and what it means when a community's own record makes the community look bad.
- [[The Persecution Template]], the fixed script that persecution stories run on, turned into a literal checklist and applied to two texts.
- [[Myth Versus the Cross]], both accounts stripped to their frame, so you can see exactly which line changes.
- [[The Test of Absence]], the strongest forensic move, which is not about what a text says but about the version that should exist and does not.
- [[The Spectrum of Legibility]], founding texts arranged on a scale of how completely they bury the body.

If you want the version where I do state the verdict, that is [[The Crucifixion Satan Never Expected]].
