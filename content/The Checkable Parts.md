title: The Checkable Parts
---

There is a way of using archaeology in these arguments that I have come to dislike, and I used to do it. You collect the finds, you stack them up, you produce the number, twenty five thousand discoveries confirming the Bible, and you finish with a line about how the spade never contradicts scripture. It feels overwhelming while you are saying it. Then someone who knows the material asks you one question about the conquest of Canaan and the whole stack goes over, because you were never making an argument. You were making a pile.

So I want to do this slowly and claim much less.

## What the evidence can actually carry

Start with the skeptical position in its strong form, because the weak form is not worth answering. The strong form is not that the Gospels are lies. It is this. They are theological documents, written decades after the events, by people with a religious stake, in a genre that tolerates invention, quite possibly far from where the events happened and long after anyone who saw them was dead. Documents like that are not history. They are what a community came to believe, written backward into a narrative.

Notice that this is not one claim. It is a bundle, and the pieces come apart.

Archaeology does not touch the resurrection. It cannot. There is no find that could confirm it and it is a category error to look for one. It does not show that Jesus is God, it does not adjudicate a miracle, and anyone selling it that way is selling something else.

What archaeology touches is the *distance* claim inside the bundle. Whether these writers were where they said they were, in the period they said, with the kind of knowledge you only have from being there.

That is a smaller claim than the one apologists usually want. It is also the one the skeptical bundle actually depends on, which is why it is worth the work.

## The kind of detail that matters

Not all confirmations are worth the same, and the ones that get quoted most are usually worth the least.

Finding that Pontius Pilate existed is nearly worthless as evidence. Of course a provincial governor existed. Nobody serious thought otherwise, and a forger writing in 150 AD could have got the name from Tacitus or Josephus.

What matters is the class of detail a forger cannot get right. Details that were true only in one place, only for a short window, and that left no trace in the literature a later writer would have access to. Those are the ones where accuracy has only one explanation.

The Pilate inscription found at Caesarea in 1961 is useful for exactly one line of it, and it is not the name. It gives his title as prefect. The Gospels use *hegemon*, the general term for a governor, and Roman practice in Judaea shifted from prefect to procurator around the middle of the century, after which later writers, including Tacitus, refer to Pilate with the later word. The Gospel usage is compatible with the earlier title. The name is nothing. The administrative register is something.

The same test sorts the rest.

**Luke's technical vocabulary.** In Acts 17:6 he calls the officials of Thessalonica *politarchs*, a title that appears in no classical author and was long treated as a mistake, until inscriptions from Macedonia turned up using exactly that word for exactly those officials. In Acts 16:12 he calls Philippi's administrative division a *meris*, again attacked as wrong for Macedonia, and again confirmed by documentary evidence of Macedonian usage. In Acts 28:7 he calls Publius the *protos* of Malta, first man, an unusual local title confirmed by Maltese inscriptions. Herod Agrippa I he calls king, where nearly every comparable ruler in the region should be called tetrarch, and Agrippa is in fact the one who held the royal title.

Take those individually and each is a curiosity. Take them together and you have a writer who is right about local administrative nomenclature in four separate provinces, on points where the standard educated guess would have been wrong, and where being wrong would have been invisible to his readers and to everyone else for eighteen centuries. Nobody gets that pattern by luck and nobody researches it from a library in another country a century later, because the information was not in any library. It was on stones in those towns.

**Acts 17:18**, where Athenian philosophers call Paul *spermologos*, seed-picker, which the NIV renders as babbler. The word is not biblical vocabulary. It is local Athenian abuse with a specific intellectual pedigree. Using it correctly in the mouths of the right people in the right city is the kind of thing you cannot fake and would have no reason to attempt.

**The pools.** John 5:2 describes a pool near the Sheep Gate with five covered colonnades, and the five was for a long time read as symbolism rather than architecture, on the reasonable grounds that pools do not have five sides. Excavation near St. Anne's found a double pool with colonnades on the four sides and one across the dividing partition. The Pool of Siloam in John 9 was identified with a small later basin until construction work in 2004 exposed a large stepped Second Temple pool nearby. In both cases the specific architectural claim, the one that looked like invention, is the one that turned out to be a description.

**The Gallio inscription** at Delphi is the most useful single object in the list and has nothing to do with theology. It fixes the proconsulship of Gallio to a known year, which fixes Paul's appearance before him in Acts 18:12 to a window of months, which anchors the chronology of everything around it. That is not a confirmation of a story. It is a calendar peg, and it is why we can date Paul at all.

**Crucifixion practice.** The 1968 discovery at Giv'at ha-Mivtar of an ossuary containing the remains of a crucified man, Yehohanan, with an iron nail still through the heel bone and a fragment of wood attached, settled two things that had been genuinely uncertain. That crucifixion in first century Judaea involved nailing, and, more to the point, that a crucified man could be taken down and given ordinary family burial in a rock-cut tomb. The second is the one that bears on the accounts, because the burial has often been challenged on the grounds that Rome left bodies on crosses. Sometimes it did. Here is a case where it did not.

## What I am cutting, and why

An honest version of this argument has to say what it is throwing away, so here is mine.

**The James ossuary**, inscribed as James son of Joseph brother of Jesus. Declared a forgery by the Israel Antiquities Authority, the owner acquitted in 2012 after a long trial, scholars still split, and the object has no excavation provenance because it came through the antiquities market. An object without provenance cannot do evidential work no matter how the trial went. Set it aside entirely.

**The Nazareth Inscription**, the imperial edict threatening death for grave robbery, which apologists like to place in Nazareth just after the crucifixion and read as Rome reacting to the empty tomb story. I used to like this one. It does not survive scrutiny. Its provenance is unknown, it was acquired on the market and only said to be from Nazareth, and recent isotope work on the marble points to a quarry that makes an Aegean origin more likely than a Galilean one. Even granting everything, the connection to the resurrection is speculation with no evidence attaching it to the event. Drop it.

**The number.** Twenty five thousand finds confirming the Bible is not a real figure with a real methodology behind it, and Nelson Glueck's line about no archaeological discovery ever having controverted a biblical reference is quoted far past what Glueck meant and far past what is true. Do not use either.

**Zero contradictions.** This is the claim that ruins the argument, because it is false and the person you are talking to knows it is false. The archaeology of the Exodus at the scale the text describes is a real problem. The conquest narrative in Joshua does not line up cleanly with the occupation history of several of the named cities. Some numbers in Chronicles are not credible. These are live disputes with serious people on multiple sides, and none of them are answered by pretending they do not exist.

What survives all of that cutting is narrower and, I think, harder to move. The New Testament material in particular, and Luke and John specifically, is accurate on a dense body of local, temporary, checkable detail of a kind that is not available at a distance.

## The move that is actually available

So here is the argument, stated with the limits on.

You cannot hold that a writer was fantastically well informed about everything checkable and freely inventive about everything uncheckable, *unless you can say what mechanism separates the two*. That is the whole thing. It is not a proof and it does not conclude to the resurrection. It is a demand for a principled line.

And there is one. It is worth stating in its best form, because it is the answer I would give if I were on the other side. A writer can be scrupulously accurate about the world and still be committed to a theological claim he did not personally verify, in the same way that a careful journalist can report a town's geography perfectly and its rumours credulously. Genre governs which parts are load-bearing. Accuracy about pools is simply not the same faculty as accuracy about resurrections.

I think that is a serious answer and I do not think it is a complete one. It works well against a general appeal to reliability. It works less well against the specific finding here, which is not that these writers were careful in general but that they had access no distant later writer had. The politarchs and the *meris* and the seed-picker are not evidence of care. They are evidence of proximity. And proximity is precisely the thing the skeptical bundle needed to deny, because a legend requires distance from witnesses in order to form.

That is what the checkable parts buy, and it is all they buy. Not the empty tomb. The removal of one specific escape route, the one where these documents get written far away and long after by people who would not have known a Macedonian district from a Judaean one.

Whether the people standing that close were telling the truth about what they saw is a different argument, and I make it elsewhere.
