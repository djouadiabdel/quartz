---
title: The Commandment You Cannot Obey
---

> *You shall not covet your neighbor's house. You shall not covet your neighbor's wife, nor his male or female servant, nor his ox or donkey, nor anything that belongs to your neighbor.* (Exodus 20:17)

Read the Ten Commandments again and notice something strange about the last one. Nine of them forbid an act. Do not kill. Do not steal. Do not commit adultery. Do not bear false witness. You can point to the moment each of them is broken, there is a body, a missing object, a bed, a lie. Then the tenth reaches past the act and grabs the thing behind it. It does not forbid the theft. It forbids the wanting. [It is the only commandment aimed not at your hands but at the engine that moves them.]

That should already stop you. Every other law waits for you to do something. This one convicts you while you are still sitting still. Girard saw it too, and it is where his whole reading of the Bible begins: the sixth through ninth commandments forbid acts of violence in order of seriousness, brief and simple, and then the tenth breaks the pattern. It is the longest of them, and instead of an act it names a state of the heart.

## Watch the lawgiver think

Here is the thing most people miss. You can almost watch the tenth commandment being written.

It starts as a list. House, wife, servant, ox, donkey. The lawgiver is trying to name every object people fight over, and somewhere in the middle of the list he realizes it is hopeless. The objects never run out, and they keep changing from one generation to the next. So he does something the other nine commandments never do. He abandons the objects and turns to the one thing in the equation that never changes: *anything that belongs to your neighbor.*

[That last phrase is the whole discovery.] The problem was never the ox. An ox standing in an empty field means nothing to you. It becomes desirable the instant it is *his*. The neighbor is what makes the object glow. Strip the list away and the sentence confesses its own secret: you do not desire objects directly, you desire them through people. Your neighbor is the model for your desire, and the model is what turns a plain object into a prize.

That is mimetic desire, and the Bible names it here, at the foot of Sinai, three thousand years before psychology had a word for it. We tell ourselves desire runs on a straight line, me and the thing I want. The tenth commandment says there is always a third point on that line, the person through whom the thing became worth wanting. And once two people are pointed at the same object by the same wanting, you do not have desire anymore. You have a collision waiting to happen.

## The word that reaches all the way back to Eden

Now go to the Hebrew, because the English hides the sharpest part.

The verb is *chamad*. Our translations render it "covet," and that word does damage, because "covet" sounds like a rare and lurid sin, the special vice of misers and adulterers. *Chamad* means nothing so narrow. It means, simply, *to desire, to delight in, to find precious.* It is an ordinary word for an ordinary hunger. Which is exactly Girard's point: the Decalogue would not spend its longest commandment forbidding some marginal perversion. The desire it forbids is the desire of everyone. It is desire as such.

And here is where it opens up. *Chamad* is the word used of Eve. In Genesis, the tree is "good for food, and a *delight* to the eyes, and *to be desired* to make one wise" (Genesis 3:6). The same root that forbids coveting at Sinai describes the first reach of the first hand toward the first forbidden thing. Deuteronomy's version of the commandment even doubles it, stacking *chamad* next to a second craving-verb, *'avah*, the root behind the wilderness "graves of craving" where Israel's appetite killed it. The tenth commandment is not a new rule dropped from a mountain. It is Sinai reaching back across the whole story and putting a name to what went wrong in the garden. [The commandment and the Fall use the same word, because they are about the same wound.]

And once you hold that word, the Old Testament stops reading like a rulebook and starts reading like a case file:

- **Cain and Abel.** The first death in the Bible is not a robbery, it is a mimetic rivalry. Two brothers, the same altar, the same longing for the same regard, and when the difference between them collapses, God warns Cain in almost clinical language: *sin is crouching at your door; its desire is for you, but you must rule over it* (Genesis 4:7). He does not rule it. The wanting becomes the murder.
- **Achan.** When Israel's first conquest goes wrong, the confession is the tenth commandment in three verbs: *I saw... I coveted (chamad)... I took* (Joshua 7:21). Desire, then seizure, in one breath.
- **Ahab and Naboth.** A king wants his neighbor's vineyard, the neighbor will not sell, and so the king's people frame the neighbor with false witnesses and stone him (1 Kings 21). Read it slowly and you watch coveting turn into the exact machinery of the scapegoat: an innocent man accused, a crowd assembled, a body in a field, and a powerful man's desire satisfied over it.
- **David and Bathsheba.** He covets the neighbor's wife and the tenth commandment cascades straight down through the seventh and the sixth, adultery then murder, until the prophet Nathan traps him with a parable about a rich man who could not stand to let a poor man keep his one small lamb (2 Samuel 11-12).
- **The prophet's summary.** Micah says it flat, and he uses the commandment's own vocabulary: *they covet (chamad) fields and seize them, and houses, and take them away* (Micah 2:2). Covet, seize, take. The chain never varies.

[The tenth commandment is not one law among ten. It is the diagnosis the other nine are treating symptoms of.] Murder, theft, adultery, false witness: run them back upstream and every one of them starts as a wanting that came through a neighbor.

## The trap Paul saw, and why God forbade it anyway

So the Bible has found the engine of human violence. It forbids it. Problem solved.

Except it isn't, and the New Testament says so out loud.

> *I would not have known what it was to covet if the law had not said, "You shall not covet."* (Romans 7:7)

Paul is admitting something almost embarrassing. The law naming the desire is what woke the desire up. [Forbid a thing and you have just pointed at it.] The prohibition is negative by nature, and the negative provokes the very hunger it meant to starve. Girard says the same: the trouble with prohibitions is that they do not finally do their job, because their negative character stirs the mimetic urge to break them. Tell a room full of people not to look at the door and watch what they look at.

Which raises the honest question, the one you should actually ask. If God knew the prohibition would inflame the very thing it forbids, why give it at all?

Two answers, and they fit together.

First, the prohibition was never meant to be the cure. It was meant to be the diagnosis. Girard is careful here, and so is Paul. Jesus does not scorn the Law, he says he fulfills it, and he knows, unlike our modern despisers of every rule, that to hold off conflict *you have to begin with prohibitions.* Begin. Not end. The commandment is the first move, not the last one. Its job is to drag the disease into the light where it can be named, and a disease you cannot name you cannot treat. This is exactly how Paul frames it: *the law was our guardian until Christ came* (Galatians 3:24), and, more bluntly, *the law came in so that the trespass might increase, but where sin increased, grace abounded all the more* (Romans 5:20). The commandment turns the volume up on the sickness on purpose. It makes covetousness undeniable, so that when the cure arrives you already know what it is curing.

Second, and this is the part the whole essay turns on: the commandment leaves a hole in the exact shape of its own solution. It forbids you to take your model from your neighbor, and it does not give you another model to take instead. It says *do not desire through him* and falls silent about whom to desire through. A law that forbids a wanting cannot fill the hunger underneath it. Only a different object of imitation can do that. [The tenth commandment is a question the Old Testament asks and cannot answer. It needs a Person to walk on and finish the sentence.]

## Desire was never the disease

Before we get to the answer, kill the wrong version of it, because almost everyone reaches for it. They read all this and conclude that desire itself is the sickness, that holiness means wanting nothing, emptying out, going cold. That is not it, and getting this wrong ruins everything downstream.

We are the creature that traded instinct for desire. An animal knows what it wants, its wanting is wired shut by nature, a fixed set of needs and the reflexes to meet them. We do not have that. Once our basic needs are met we go on wanting, intensely, and we do not know what we want, because no instinct is left to tell us. So we borrow. We look to the people around us and we catch their wanting the way you catch a yawn. [This is not our corruption. This is the exact thing that makes us human and not animal.] To desire through another is simply to be a person. It is the open space where love, art, worship, and ambition all become possible. It is also the open space where rivalry and murder become possible. Same door.

So the gospel is not going to ask you to stop desiring, that would be asking you to stop being human. Mimesis is not the Fall. Mimesis is the equipment. The Fall is a mimesis pointed at the wrong model. Which means the entire question, the whole of it, was never *whether* you will imitate. You will, you cannot help it, you are built for it. The only question left standing is *whom.*

## One model, and why it actually works

The tenth commandment, read this way, is quietly pointing at the answer it cannot give.

If your neighbor is your model, you are locked into rivalry, and not by accident. You both come to want the same object, and the object cannot be split. Two hands on one ox is war, and the war escalates on its own: the more he refuses to let go, the more you want it, and your wanting revives his, even after his had died. This is why the same Torah that diagnoses the disease immediately prescribes the strange arithmetic of *love your neighbor as yourself* (Leviticus 19:18), neither more nor less, because to worship your neighbor is to end by hating him.

The way out is not a better rule against wanting. It is a model who stands outside the rivalry entirely.

> *Be imitators of God, as beloved children.* (Ephesians 5:1)

And now you have to explain *why* that works, because "just imitate God" sounds like a slogan until you see the mechanism. It works for one precise reason: **God does not desire the way a rival desires.** Listen to how Jesus describes the Father: he *makes his sun rise on the evil and on the good, and sends rain on the just and the unjust* (Matthew 5:45). He gives without counting, without measuring one person against another, without hoarding. His desire is not acquisitive. It does not need to win, because there is nothing it can lose.

That is the hinge. When you imitate a neighbor, you inherit his grasping, and grasping runs into other grasping and detonates. When you imitate a Father whose wanting is pure generosity, there is nothing to collide over. What God "wants" is not a scarce object two people must fight for. It is the good of the other, and the good of the other is infinitely shareable. [You cannot both own the ox. You can both have God, and neither of you has less because the other has him.] Copy a rival and his desire becomes your prison. Copy the one whose desire is self-giving, and imitation stops producing rivalry at the root, because the thing being imitated was never a hunger to possess.

There is one more turn, and Girard catches it. When Jesus says *imitate me*, he is not offering himself as a self-made original the way our gurus do, the great one who imitates no one. He says the opposite: *the Son can do nothing of his own accord, but only what he sees the Father doing* (John 5:19). He is a model who is himself an imitator. So in inviting you to imitate him, he invites you to imitate *his* imitation, to look where he is looking. He is a model who is also a window. He never becomes the wall you crash into, because he is always pointing past himself. That is the whole shape of good mimesis in one image: not a mirror you fight with, but a window you look through.

## The model made human, and why the Incarnation is the answer

Here is the problem that has been waiting the whole time, and it is a real one, not a technicality.

*Be imitators of God* is the right instruction and an impossible one. You imitate what you can watch. You cannot watch pure transcendence. You cannot copy a Being you have never seen do a single human thing, in a single human situation, with a body like yours. The model is correct and the model is out of reach. And this is not a small gap you can will your way across. It is the entire distance between heaven and a person standing in a field wanting his neighbor's ox.

So the Word became flesh. And the astonishing thing is that when Athanasius argues for *why* the Incarnation had to happen, seventeen centuries ago and with no thought of mimetic theory, he gives our exact reason.

Athanasius asks: could God not have just fixed us from a distance? Could creation not have taught us, or a prophet, or simple repentance? And he answers no, on grounds that land precisely where our argument lands.

First, no human model could do it, because every human model is himself warped. *You cannot put straight in others what is crooked in yourself*, he writes, and even the best of men were *confused and blinded* by the same disease they would have to cure. In mimetic terms: every neighbor you might copy is already inside the rivalry, already distorted, already a rival in waiting. A cure cannot come from within the sickness. The model has to come from outside the whole system, and there is only one such place.

Second, and this is the line that should stop you, Athanasius says God took to himself *a human body* so that *men should recognize him in the part who could not do so in the whole,* so that *those who could not lift their eyes to his unseen power might recognize and behold him in the likeness of themselves. For, being men, they would naturally learn to know his Father more quickly and directly by means of a body that corresponded to their own.* Read that again with the tenth commandment in your hand. The Father is the model you cannot reach. So the model comes *in the likeness of yourself*, in a body that corresponds to yours, precisely so you finally have something you *can* imitate. That is not a devotional flourish. That is Athanasius giving the same answer we just reached: the right model was unimitable, so he made himself imitable.

And then the image that ties it in a bow. Athanasius says the likeness of God in humanity was like a portrait painted on a panel that has been stained and obliterated. What do you do with a defaced portrait? You do not throw out the panel. *The subject of the portrait has to come and sit for it again, and then the likeness is re-drawn on the same material.* The Image of the Father came and sat, in the flesh, on our own panel, so the image could be copied off the Original once more. [That is "the model made human" stated as a doctrine of the Incarnation four hundred years before anyone said "mimetic." The image is restored only when the Subject himself comes and sits to be copied.]

This is why the Incarnation is not an add-on to the argument. It *is* the argument. The tenth commandment found the engine of violence and could only forbid it. The gospel does not add an eleventh prohibition. It sends the one Model who is human enough to copy and generous enough never to become a rival, and it says the thing the whole Old Testament was reaching for and could not say: not *stop wanting*, but *want like this, want with me, look where I am looking.*

> *Who, though he was in the form of God, did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped.* (Philippians 2:6)

There is the tenth commandment turned inside out and made flesh. The command says: do not grasp at what is your neighbor's. And here is the one being who was genuinely offered the highest thing there is, equality with God, and *did not grasp.* Covetousness is the reach that seizes. He is the reach that lets go, all the way down to a cross. He is not a man carefully keeping the tenth commandment. He is the tenth commandment answered in a person, the anti-covetous One, the model whose whole life is the refusal to grab.

## Why it had to stay hidden: the scandal, the powers, the dragon

Now push on the part that unsettles people. If this is the answer, why is it delivered in the dark? Why the secrecy, why the slowness, why does almost no one in the story understand what is happening while it happens?

Because of how the mechanism works. Girard's word for the trap is *skandalon*, the stumbling block, the obstacle that repels and attracts at once. Scandals breed scandals, rivalries pull in bystanders, and the whole social body drifts toward the same crisis it always drifts toward: everyone against one. The crowd finds a victim, discharges its violence onto him, and feels peace return. Then it tells itself the victim deserved it. And crucially, the mechanism only works *as long as it stays invisible.* The crowd has to believe the victim was truly guilty. The moment the machine is seen clearly, the moment the victim is known to be innocent, it stops producing peace and starts producing shame. Exposure is death to it.

This is why the New Testament keeps insisting the powers did not understand.

> *None of the rulers of this age understood this, for if they had, they would not have crucified the Lord of glory.* (1 Corinthians 2:8)

Read that as Girard reads it. Satan is not first a red figure with horns, he is the whole accusing mechanism, the accuser, the prosecutor of the victim, and also the false order that the lynching restores. Which is why the Gospels can ask the riddle *how can Satan cast out Satan?* The prince of this world is both the violence and the machine that expels the violence, and the machine runs on concealment. So when the powers move against Jesus, they are running their oldest, most reliable program: find the one, blame the one, kill the one, and get your peace. They have done it since the foundation of the world and it has always worked.

Except this time the victim is provably innocent, and this time he rises, and the machine, for the first time, is caught in the open with the light on. *Having disarmed the powers and authorities, he made a public spectacle of them, triumphing over them by the cross* (Colossians 2:15). Notice the logic, it is the same as before: the powers are not put on display because they are defeated, they are defeated *because they are put on display.* The dragon of Revelation, *that ancient serpent, who is called the devil and Satan, the deceiver of the whole world* (Revelation 12:9), is undone not by a bigger army but by being seen. [The Cross does not beat the mechanism at its own game. It turns the lights on in the room where the game is played, and the game cannot survive being watched.]

So it had to stay hidden, because the whole point was to let the mechanism run all the way to the end, on a victim who would not fit, so that for once the machine would complete its cycle and *fail to close.* If the rulers had understood, they would have stopped, and the thing would never have been exposed. The secret is not God hiding the good news. The secret is God letting the lie finish its sentence so it could be caught in the act.

## The blindness of the disciples, and why it is not a flaw

Which brings us to the detail people treat as an embarrassment: the disciples don't get it. Right through the story they misunderstand, they argue about who is greatest, which is the mimetic rivalry sitting in the middle of the twelve, they scatter, Peter denies. They do not understand the cross until after the resurrection. And I want to say plainly: this is not a bug in the account. It is the account working exactly as it must.

For one thing, it is honest. The disciples are human beings, and human beings are inside the mechanism, all of them, which is the whole thesis. If the followers had been serenely above it all, the story would be lying about what people are. Their blindness is the thesis holding even for the men closest to Jesus.

For another, and this is the sharper point, their blindness is what proves the account is not just another myth. A myth is written by the winners, from inside the machine, and it never doubts that the victim was guilty and the crowd was right, because the people telling it never saw the machine at all. If the disciples had understood the mechanism in advance and coolly narrated it, you would suspect a constructed story, a theology dressed as an event. Instead you get men who were as blind as everyone else, who fled, who only saw what had happened *after* it broke over them, and who then wrote down both their own failure and the innocence of the one they abandoned. Girard puts the sequence exactly: the Paraclete, the advocate, the lawyer for the defense, reveals the innocence of the victim *to the disciples first, and then to all of us.* The understanding comes from outside them and after the fact, which is precisely why it does not read like propaganda. [A myth cannot see its own victim. The Gospel is the one story told by people who were on the wrong side of the machine and lived to describe it.]

Their blindness, in other words, is not weakness in the case. It is the chain of custody on the evidence.

## The secret in one sentence

Put it all together.

The tenth commandment is the deepest of the ten because it is the only one that reaches the root, desire itself, the wanting that comes through a neighbor and turns the whole world into a field of rivalries. And precisely because it reaches that far, it hits the wall no law can climb: you cannot forbid a heart into peace. The diagnosis is perfect and the prohibition is powerless, and that is not a failure of the commandment. It is the commandment telling you what has to come next.

What comes next is not an eleventh rule. It is a Person you are told to copy. The Father was the right model and the unreachable one, so the Image came and sat on our own panel to be copied off the Original again, human enough to imitate and self-giving enough never to become a rival. He let the ancient machine run all the way to its end on his own innocent body, so that for once it would be caught in the light and fail to close, and he left the telling of it to men who had been as blind as the crowd, so that no one could mistake it for the crowd's own myth.

Mimesis was always the human condition. We were always going to imitate someone. Every rivalry, every escalation, every quiet war between neighbors comes from imitating the wrong model. The gospel does not abolish imitation, it could not, it would be abolishing us. It gives you the one model who never becomes your rival, because everything he wants can be shared without anyone having less.

The tenth commandment found the engine of human violence. Jesus is what you put in its place. [Change your model and you have changed everything, and that, in one sentence, is the whole secret of desire.]
