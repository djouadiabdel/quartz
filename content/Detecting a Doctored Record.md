---
title: Detecting a Doctored Record
---

*Case File 01. Textual forensics.*

How do you spot a story that has been rewritten?

Not by faith, and not by piety about which texts are trustworthy. By the same tells a detective uses on a doctored confession. There are only a few of them, they are boring, and they work.

## The rule

Start with the thing everyone forgets about ancient literature. Almost every story that survives from the ancient world survives because somebody kept it. Papyrus rots, cities burn, libraries get sacked. A text lasts only if a community copies it, generation after generation, at real cost, because that community wants it kept.

Which means the survival of any ancient story is already a fact about its keepers, not only about its contents. You are never reading the past directly. You are reading what somebody chose to carry forward.

And the people who do the carrying are, overwhelmingly, the people who came out on top. The story gets told over the body of whoever they killed, by the side that did the killing, to the descendants of the side that did the killing.

## The tell

Once you know that, the fingerprint of tampering is easy to state.

A doctored record flatters its keepers.

That is the whole tell. The survivors come out clean. Their motives were sound, their hands were forced, they did what anyone would have done. The dead come out guilty. He brought it on himself, he had it coming, the community had no choice. The gap between how the keepers look and how the victim looks is the fingerprint, and the wider that gap, the more editing you are looking at.

This is not a rule about ancient texts specifically. It is a rule about documents in general. Read a police department's internal report on a shooting by one of its officers. Read a government's account of a massacre it carried out. Read any organization's own account of its worst day. You already know how to do this. You have read a company statement about a scandal and known, without being able to prove a single line false, that the document was written by people protecting themselves. That instinct is a real forensic skill. It has a shape, and the shape is: the record makes its keepers look better than people usually are.

## The odd one

Now here is a text that a community copied for centuries, at real cost, under real pressure.

It calls its own founders cowards who ran. Every one of them, at the moment it counted, gone.

It says the leader of that group, the one the community afterward organized itself around, stood in a courtyard and swore three times that he had never met the man. Not a moment of weakness glossed over in a subordinate clause. The scene is told at length, with the detail of the servant girl who pushed him and the sound that broke him.

It records the group arguing, more than once, about which of them was the greatest, while the man they followed was walking toward his execution.

And the first witnesses it produces for its central claim are women, in a culture whose own courts would not have accepted their testimony.

## The keepers wrote themselves guilty

Put those four facts next to the rule and something is wrong.

A community that edits a record edits it in one direction. There is no mystery about which direction. Every incentive runs the same way, and the incentives do not have to be conscious or cynical. Memory itself drifts toward self-protection. You do not need a conspiracy to explain why groups end up with flattering accounts of themselves. You need a conspiracy to explain the opposite.

The people who kept this text had the same incentives as everyone else, the same centuries to work with, and the same freedom to smooth it out. The cowardice is not load-bearing. Nothing in the claim they were making required their founders to look bad. They could have fixed it. It would have taken very little.

They did not fix it.

## The question

So the case file closes on the question it opened with, sharpened.

Who edits the record to make themselves the villain?
