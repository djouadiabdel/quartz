title: The Rule Girard Actually Wrote
---

*Part 3 of eleven. The series is [[Something That Can Say No]].*

Every page number in this project came out of the project's own files, and not one of them had ever been checked against a copy of the book.

I want to be exact about what that means. The lexicons were built from quotations. The quotations came from notes. The notes came from reading, months earlier, of books I did not have open. Eight rounds of measurement, four significant figures deep, resting on a chain nobody had walked back.

So I walked it back. All four books turned out to be on my own disk.

Two quotations came back exact, word for word. Two needed correcting.

A page number was wrong by one. And a chapter attribution was wrong, because the chapter enumerating the stereotypes stops at three. The fourth is named at the head of the next chapter. So the citation should have read *chapters 2 to 3* for eight rounds, and did not.

None of that is interesting. This is.

**Both of the verified quotations had an intervening sentence removed with no marker.** The words that are there are Girard's. A reader comparing my quotation to the page finds a sentence in the book that is not in my version, and nothing on the line telling them to expect one. I did not do it to change the meaning and it does not change the meaning. It is still the thing I would be angriest about if somebody did it to me.

And then, four pages into chapter 3 of *The Scapegoat*, was the sentence this post is about.

## The rule

This is tier V. The book is on the shelf. Go and check it.

> "Each time an oral or written testament mentions an act of violence that is directly or indirectly collective we question whether it includes the description of a social and cultural crisis... The juxtaposition of more than one stereotype within a single document indicates persecution. **Not all the stereotypes must be present: three are enough and often even two.**"

That is Girard's own decision rule for identifying a persecution text. It is the procedure he uses, stated by him, in the book where he lays out the stereotypes.

Read the last sentence again and put it on the ladder from [[Girard in the Minimum|post 2]].

| level | what it asks | who requires it |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | the vocabulary appears | nobody |
| **2** | **two or three of four, anywhere in the document** | **Girard, *Scapegoat* ch. 3** |
| 2 strict | all four, anywhere in the document | true in 90% of texts |
| **3** | all four **on one passage** | **this project, and nobody else** |

Girard's procedure sits on the rung [[Girard in the Minimum|post 2]] has just shown is nearly worthless. And it sits **below** the strict version, because it does not ask for four. It asks for two.

Now let the project's own measurement meet it.

All four buckets fire in **90% of 72 Greek and Roman texts**, including texts with no persecution in them anywhere. If all four are present in nine texts out of ten, then *three are enough and often even two* classifies almost the whole of Greek literature as a persecution document.

Nobody invented that objection. It is Girard's stated rule standing next to a number this project produced, and the number was produced without anyone noticing it bore on the rule.

## Two things that do not follow

**It does not follow that Girard is wrong.**

Say the defence at full strength, because it is the strongest available and it is probably correct.

A rule of thumb for reading a medieval chronicle is not a claim about what the mechanism produces. Girard can hold that the event generates all four marks and that a surviving text preserves only some. Then a reader's rule sensibly asks for less than the event contains.

They are different jobs. One is forensics on a damaged document. The other is a claim about the machine that damaged it.

I think that is right. I also think it does not get him all the way out, because this project's task **is** the reader's task in that sentence. Deciding, from a document, whether persecution is being described. That is what my instrument does, on the object his rule is written for.

**And it does not follow that the project wasted its time.** The opposite, and this is the part that changes what every later failure means.

> **Level 3 is my invention.** Girard never asked for co-location on a passage.

For eight rounds the instrument was testing something **harder than its source requires**, and failing at it.

So at the end of [[Ten Rounds and the Half That Never Carried Anything|post 9]] the tempting sentence is *Girard's claim did not show up*. The honest sentence is longer and says two things at once. The strengthened version of his claim did not show up. And the version he actually stated is refuted by this project's own 90% figure, in the other direction, for being too easy to satisfy.

Hold both. Neither is comfortable and both are checkable.

## Run it against the case it is supposed to distinguish

Here is the move that settles it, and it is the question to ask of any detection rule anyone ever hands you.

> **Point it at the thing it is meant to tell apart.**

Girard's rule says two or three stereotypes in a document indicate persecution. So point it at the Passion. The arrest, the trial before the Jewish authorities and then Pilate, the crowd calling for execution, the crucifixion.

That is the one text his whole theory has to treat differently. It is the right target for a persecution detector.

**Crisis.** A city under occupation, a festival crowd, an authority in open conflict with a popular teacher.

**Crimes.** Blasphemy, which is Girard's third crime family exactly.

**Marks.** A provincial and a carpenter's son who is simultaneously a claimed king. Too low and too high at once, both directions in one person.

**Violence.** A crowd calling out together, and a public execution.

Four of four. Emphatically, not grudgingly.

The rule fires on the Gospels as hard as it fires on anything. And by its own terms it must, because Girard says the Gospels describe the same event.

> **A rule that classifies both sides of your distinction as positive is not a distinction.**

## And Girard knows this, which is what turns a gotcha into a finding

He never claims the four stereotypes separate myth from Gospel. He puts the difference somewhere else entirely, in one sentence, and it is the sharpest thing he ever wrote.

*I See Satan Fall Like Lightning*, page 3, verified word for word:

> "not merely the innocence of the victims versus their guilt, but the fact that, in mythology, **no one ever questions this guilt**. In the Gospels, the revealing account of scapegoating emanates not from the unanimous crowd but from a dissenting few. Initially, Jesus' disciples almost surrender to the mimetic power of the many, but on the third day, thanks to the Resurrection, they secede from the deluded mob and proclaim the innocence of their Lord. **In mythology no dissenting voice is ever heard.**"

Point at the load-bearing half, because it is routinely misread and the misreading cost this project four rounds.

**The differentia is not that the victims are innocent.** Girard rules that out himself, in the same sentence. *Not merely the innocence of the victims versus their guilt.*

**The differentia is that no one ever questions.**

It is a claim about the crowd's verdict, not about the victim's character. Those are two different things, and the distance between them is where most of this project's null results live.

## The detector and the discriminator

So the four stereotypes were never the discriminating instrument.

> **They are the detector.** They tell you a persecution is being described. They cannot tell you who is describing it, or whether the describer knows what he is doing.

Which gives the finding its real shape. Not that Girard contradicts himself. That the first three rounds built an ever better detector and tested it as though it were a discriminator.

Round 4 is the first round in the project that builds the discriminator. It does it by adding a fifth bucket, for dissent, because that is where Girard put the difference.

The theory said the four stereotypes could not do the job. The sentence saying so was on the shelf the whole time, in a book I owned, and could have been read before any of it.

## What the check cost, and what it bought

Four books, one afternoon, and a command-line tool that extracts text from a PDF. That is the entire method. There is no scholarship in it and no expertise required.

What it bought:

| | |
|---|---|
| quotations verified word for word | 2 |
| corrections to page or chapter | 2 |
| **unmarked elisions found in my own quotations** | **2** |
| decision rules found that nobody had read | **1** |
| rounds of measurement that turn out to be aimed one rung too high | **8** |

And there is a second find in the same afternoon that belongs to [[The Other Two Instruments|post 11]] rather than here. The conversion instrument scores three transformations. The page it is built from lists **seven**. The selection of which three was never written down anywhere, in any file, and nobody noticed until somebody opened the book.

Two instruments. Same afternoon. Same cause.

> **Both were built from a memory of a page rather than from the page.**

The uncomfortable arithmetic is that the citation check took less time than any single round of scoring, and it moved more than most of them did.

## Why this happens, and it is not stupidity

**A recipe you have cooked from memory for years.**

You know it. Forty times. You have taught it to somebody. You could write it out on request. Then you find the card, and it says 180 where you have always used 200, and it does not mention the step you have always thought was the whole trick.

The dish is not bad. It may genuinely be better than the card. But it is **yours**, and you have spent years attributing it to somebody else, sincerely, without ever once putting the card next to the pan.

Notice the specific thing that made it possible. The recipe was quoted from memory of a summary, and everybody in the chain was quoting the same summary.

The clinical version is citing a guideline you have never opened. You know what it says because you learned it from a senior, who learned it from a talk, which was based on the guideline. The chain is usually accurate and is never checkable at any single link. The gap between what the guideline says and what everybody knows it says widens by one small step per retelling.

That is normally harmless. It stops being harmless the moment you build an instrument out of it.

An instrument does not know it is repeating a summary. It will happily produce four significant figures on top of one.

## The ceiling on this post, and it is real

This rests on **one verified sentence.**

Girard wrote for fifty years. He may well say the opposite somewhere else, at length, with better reasons. Nobody has looked. I have not looked.

So this post is not entitled to the words *Girard's position is*, and neither is the objection it raises. One verified sentence is one verified sentence.

> **Finding one sentence is not scholarship. It is the first ten minutes of scholarship**, and a series that reports it should say so in the same breath.

There is a second thing wrong with the 90% figure as an argument, and it cuts against me rather than for me. That number comes from my lexicon, and `marks` is 44% the single word `king`. If the buckets are firing on genre rather than on persecution, then 90% is a fact about my instrument and cannot be used against Girard's rule at all.

[[Turning Four Sentences Into Code|Post 4]] builds the instrument, and that is where you find out how much of it is `king`.
